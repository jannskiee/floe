A [direct connection](/glossary#direct-connection) means the file goes from your device to theirs
with nothing in between. No server carries it, so nobody is paying for the bandwidth and Floe sets
no limit on the size. The speed is whatever the slower of your two internet connections can manage.

Most transfers are direct, including ones between a laptop on home Wi-Fi and a phone on mobile
data on the other side of the country. Some mobile carriers' networks can force the
[relay](/glossary#relay) instead: see [When you get one](#when-you-get-one-and-when-you-do-not).

## How two devices on different networks find each other

Almost nothing on the internet has a public address of its own. Your laptop sits behind a router,
your phone sits behind your carrier's equipment, and neither can be dialed directly the way a web
server can.

[WebRTC](/glossary#webrtc), the technology Floe connects devices with, gets around this with a
[STUN](/glossary#stun) server, whose only job is to answer one question: "what does my address
look like from out there?" Each device asks, gets an answer, and passes that answer to the other
through Floe's [signaling server](/glossary#signaling-server). Both then start sending packets
toward the other's address at the same moment. Because each side's router has just seen an
outgoing packet to that destination, it lets the incoming one through. The two streams meet in the
middle.

This is usually called NAT hole punching, after [NAT](/glossary#nat), the address sharing a router
does. It is why a transfer between two completely different networks still says **Direct**, and
why no file data touches a server even though neither device had a reachable address to begin
with.

The STUN server only ever sees the request for an address. It never sees a file, and it is out of
the picture the moment the connection exists.

## What direct means for you

- **No size limit from Floe.** Nothing is metered, so Floe caps nothing. A browser that receives is
  still limited by what that browser can hold. See
  [Known limitations](/how-it-works/known-limitations#a-browser-receiver-is-limited-by-what-the-browser-can-hold).
- **As fast as your connection allows.** Usually the sender's upload speed is the ceiling.
- **No server carries your files.** No server sees the traffic, encrypted or otherwise, so there
  is nothing to meter. The signaling server still introduces the two devices, and that introduction
  is trusted: see
  [Known limitations](/how-it-works/known-limitations#the-signaling-server-is-trusted-to-introduce-you-honestly).

## When you get one, and when you do not

Standard home Wi-Fi, personal hotspots, and most mobile networks connect directly.

Some networks defeat hole punching. Strict corporate and university firewalls block the attempt
outright. Some mobile carriers use carrier-grade NAT, which puts thousands of customers behind one
shared address and can make the address discovered through STUN useless. In those cases Floe falls
back to a relay, if you have allowed it to: see [Relay fallback](/how-it-works/relay-connection).
The [badge](/glossary#badge) shows which route you got.

To improve your odds of a direct connection:

- Use a normal home or personal network.
- When the two devices are in the same place, put both on the same Wi-Fi network.
- Disconnect from any VPN.
- Try a personal hotspot when you are on a corporate or university network.

## How Floe decides a connection counts as direct {#how-floe-decides-a-connection-counts-as-direct}

The one rule that matters to you: a connection counts as relayed only when the route that won
runs through the relay. The rest of this section is the technical detail behind that rule.

WebRTC gathers **candidates**, which are address-and-port combinations a device might be
reachable on, and the two [peers](/glossary#peer) try pairs of them until one works. This step is
called [ICE](/glossary#ice). Candidates come in four kinds:

- `host`: an address on the local network.
- `srflx` (server-reflexive): the public address STUN reported.
- `prflx` (peer-reflexive): learned during the connectivity checks themselves.
- `relay`: an address on a [TURN](/glossary#turn) relay.

Candidates are sent as they are discovered rather than in one batch at the end, which is called
trickle ICE and cuts setup time noticeably.

Floe calls a connection relayed only when the pair that actually won has a `relay` candidate on
one side or the other. Every other outcome is direct. This is decided by the winning pair, not by
what was offered: relay candidates are usually gathered and offered too, they just did not win.

On floe.one the STUN endpoint is Cloudflare's, at `stun.cloudflare.com:3478`. When no relay
service is configured at all, which is the default for a fresh [self-hosted](/glossary#self-hosted)
instance, the signaling server hands out Google's public STUN servers instead. A self-hosted coturn
relay doubles as the STUN server for that instance.
